"Keeping your money in the bank is safe." "When interest rates rise, depositors prosper."

Decades of state-sponsored financial indoctrination have conditioned the public to believe that commercial banks are indispensable, public-spirited infrastructure.

Yet consider the empirical reality unfolding before our eyes.

Even as the Bank of Japan hikes its policy target and complementary deposit facility rate to 1.25%—with further rate normalization on the horizon—the interest rates offered on retail ordinary deposits crawl at a meager 0.1% to 0.2%. Commercial checking accounts (*Toza Yokin*), by statutory decree, remain legally pinned at exactly zero percent.

Were policy rates to rise toward 3%, as seen across Western economies, this distortion becomes undeniable. Banks vacuum up ultra-cheap or zero-cost liquidity from captive retail and corporate depositors, park those funds at the central bank's reserve facility or short-term sovereign paper, and automatically harvest expanding net interest margins driven by the lag in deposit betas.

Playwright Bertolt Brecht famously asked in *The Threepenny Opera*:  
**"What is the robbing of a bank compared to the founding of a bank?"**

To be sure, commercial banks carry genuine banking risks: maturity mismatch (borrowing short to lend long), interest rate risk from bond devaluation (as demonstrated by the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank), credit default risk, and operational overhead. Not every cent of gross spread is risk-free windfall profit.

Yet the core institutional question remains: **independent of the legitimate risks banks assume or the services they provide, statutory privileges and regulatory barriers guarantee private banks massive institutional rents that would never survive in an open, competitive market.**

## 1. Four Institutional Rents Granted by the State

Why are commercial banks granted a license to harvest spreads protected by public infrastructure that would be inconceivable for any non-financial enterprise? Modern banking rests on four state-enforced statutory prerogatives:

$$ \text{Bank Rent} = \text{Funding Advantage} + \text{Implicit Guarantee} + \text{Payment Monopoly} + \text{Regulatory Rents} - (\text{Credit \& Liquidity Costs} + \text{Cost of Capital} + \text{Operating Costs}) $$

### ① The Two-Tier Architecture of Central Bank Access
The root asymmetry begins at the perimeter: **who is legally permitted to hold central bank money?**
Only chartered commercial banks and designated financial institutions may hold master accounts at the Bank of Japan, the Federal Reserve, or the ECB. Non-financial corporations and ordinary citizens are barred by law.
When the central bank hikes policy rates and remunerates reserves, the privilege of collecting risk-free public returns is monopolized by private intermediaries. If businesses and individuals could hold accounts directly at the central bank, no rational actor would accept 0.1% on commercial deposits. The state enforces this two-tier barrier, allowing banks to siphon off the spread.

### ② Free-Riding on Public Credit via Deposit Insurance
Banks market themselves as "rock-solid" not because of inherent balance-sheet superiority, but solely because **the state guarantees deposits (up to 10 million yen or $250,000, with full protection for non-interest settlement accounts)**.
In effect, banks socialize their creditworthiness by leveraging the ultimate fiscal taxing authority of the public. They raise capital cheaply on the back of sovereign guarantees, invest it at prevailing market rates, and privatize the spread. The public bears the tail risk, while private shareholders pocket the returns.

### ③ Credit Creation: The Exclusive License to Expand Legal Tender
As the Bank of England acknowledged in its 2014 Quarterly Bulletin, commercial banks do not merely intermediate pre-existing savings. **At the moment a loan is granted, the bank creates deposit currency out of thin air by crediting the borrower's account.**
This does not mean wealth appears from nothing without constraint—loans create offsetting assets and liabilities subject to capital adequacy ratios and default risk. Nevertheless, the power to manufacture the circulating medium of exchange of a nation is an exclusive state charter granted only to a favored corporate class.

### ④ Tollgate Control over Core Settlement Infrastructure
Direct connection to national clearing houses (such as Zengin Net and BOJ-NET) has historically been restricted to chartered banks. Regardless of how superior a fintech startup's payment technology may be, it must ultimately route through a chartered bank's tollbooth to achieve final settlement.

## 2. The Temporary Interest Rate Adjustment Law: A Statutory Cartel

The most striking empirical illustration of institutional rent in Japan is the statutory prohibition of interest on commercial checking accounts (*Toza Yokin*).

In Japan, commercial banks are legally forbidden from paying interest on checking accounts used by enterprises for trade and settlement. To analyze this anomaly, one must separate the statutory fact, its economic impact, and its stated policy rationale.

### Circumventing Antitrust via Statutory Price-Fixing
In a competitive market, banks would aggressively bid for corporate liquidity by raising checking account rates (e.g., offering 0.5% or 1.0%). For the banking sector as a whole, however, this would cause funding costs to explode and wipe out net interest margins.

Under antitrust law (the Antimonopoly Act), if commercial banks met in a backroom to collude: "Let's all agree to keep checking rates at zero percent," **they would be prosecuted for an illegal price-fixing cartel, facing severe surcharges and criminal penalties**.

Yet when **the state enacts a blanket statutory ban on checking account interest**, the banks need not collude. With zero antitrust liability, zero-cost corporate liquidity is permanently locked in across the entire banking system. Regardless of subjective intent, the objective economic effect is a **state-mandated price cartel**.

### A Relic of Wartime Mobilization
The statute enforcing this regime is the **Temporary Interest Rate Adjustment Law of 1947 (臨時金利調整法)**. Bearing the label "temporary," it has persisted for nearly 80 years.

Its institutional architecture derives from the Second World War under the National Mobilization Act: the **Bank of Japan Fund Accommodation Order (1940)** and the **Financial Business Control Order (1942)**. Designed to channel national savings into munitions production and war bonds at minimal cost, this wartime mechanism was retained post-war under the guise of "preventing destructive bank competition."

### The US Repealed Regulation Q; Japan Preserved the Ban
The United States maintained a comparable prohibition under **Regulation Q** (originating in the Banking Act of 1933). Recognizing that it penalized depositors and distorted capital allocation, **the Dodd-Frank Act fully repealed Regulation Q in July 2011**. Today, US and European institutions routinely offer interest-bearing checking accounts in competitive markets.

Japan alone preserved the zero-interest rule throughout the 1990s financial deregulation, citing "settlement stability and full deposit insurance coverage." While preventing excessive risk-taking is a standard regulatory argument, it fails to explain why enterprises must receive zero interest while banks park that same liquidity at the Bank of Japan (earning 1.25%+) to pocket risk-free rents.

### The Technical Obsolescence of Separating Accounts and the ¥350 Trillion Corporate Trap
Examining modern payment technology reveals an even more fundamental absurdity: **what technical or operational justification exists for artificially partitioning checking and savings accounts in the first place?**

In an era when paper promissory notes and physical checks were hauled by couriers to clearinghouses, maintaining a distinct non-interest ledger for paper clearances had a practical physical rationale. Yet in a modern economy where paper instruments have transitioned to electronic monetary claims and bank APIs execute multi-billion-yen settlements in sub-seconds from standard accounts, **the distinction between checking and savings is nothing more than a single boolean flag in a relational database.**

Why, then, are enterprises coerced into zero-interest checking accounts?
Regulators and bank lobbyists hide behind the defense that "checking deposits enjoy unlimited deposit insurance protection precisely because they bear zero interest." This is a classic false dichotomy that denies customer sovereignty.
If consumer and enterprise protection were the true objective, **banks would simply allow businesses to choose between an uninsured (or capped) interest-bearing account that passes through central bank yields, and a zero-interest fully guaranteed account.** As in the United States, there is zero economic rationale for statutory decrees that mandate blanket zero percent without choice.

The motive for eliminating this choice is singular: **to trap Japan's estimated ¥350 to ¥400 trillion in corporate liquidity on commercial bank balance sheets at zero cost.**
Were banks forced to pay competitive market rates (e.g., 1.0%) on this colossal pool of corporate deposits, the banking sector would face ¥3.5 to ¥4 trillion in annual interest expenses, instantly obliterating their risk-free spreads.
Artificially segregating accounts into arbitrary categories to legally expropriate yield from the corporate sector is the unvarnished institutional reality behind this obsolete dichotomy.

## 3. The Execution of Libra: Consumer Protection vs. Tollgate Defense

When private innovation attempted to challenge legacy remittance costs, how did the regulatory apparatus react? The coordinated suppression of **Facebook's (now Meta) Libra (later Diem) project in 2019** provides an instructive case study.

Backed by a user base of two billion people, Meta proposed a low-cost, borderless stablecoin backed by a basket of sovereign currencies and liquid debt. In response, global central banks, finance ministries, and regulators mounted an unprecedented offensive, ultimately terminating the initiative.

### The Fallacy of the "100-Year Regulatory Infrastructure" Defense
Defenders of legacy banking argue that "banks possess a century of mature regulatory infrastructure—deposit insurance, capital ratios, AML/CFT, and resolution frameworks—which Libra lacked."

This argument sidesteps the core issue.

If the genuine concern of regulatory authorities had been consumer protection and financial stability, the objective response was obvious:
* **Mandate 100% reserve backing in liquid sovereign assets**
* **Require segregated customer accounts and independent third-party audits**
* **Enforce strict 1:1 redemption rights**
* **Apply rigorous KYC/AML compliance**
* **Prohibit risk-bearing credit expansion**

Regulators could have established **objective technical and prudential standards: "Any entity that meets these safety criteria may access central bank settlement rails on equal terms."** With Meta's capital reserves, technical expertise, and identity infrastructure, meeting these prudential criteria was technologically and financially viable.

### Denied Standards, Absolute Exclusion
Yet regulators did not offer a conditional path to entry. They delivered an absolute, politicized shutdown.

Why? Because the threat was never whether Facebook could implement consumer protection. **The true threat was that a global technology platform would establish a parallel settlement network outside the sovereign two-tier banking architecture.**

Correspondent banking and credit card monopolies extract billions annually in cross-border fees—for what is computationally nothing more than transmitting a few kilobytes of cryptographic messages. While compliance carries real costs, it cannot justify permanent monopolistic spreads. The suppression of Libra proved that the regulatory perimeter functions not merely as consumer protection, but as an impassable moat defending the banking cartel.

## 4. The Cantillon Effect and Financialization

The critique that "finance produces no real physical goods" reflects the distortion of **financialization**—where an economy shifts from wealth creation to wealth extraction.

In the productive economy, software engineers write code, manufacturers produce goods, and farmers grow food, receiving compensation for newly created utility.
In bloated modern financial architectures, however, vast resources are dedicated to **rent extraction**: positioning tollgates along human commerce and inflating credit bubbles that demand public bailouts upon collapse.

### Regressive Redistribution via the Cantillon Effect
As the 18th-century economist Richard Cantillon demonstrated, newly created money does not diffuse evenly:
* **Those nearest the monetary spigot (central banks, megabanks, private equity, prime contractors)** receive new liquidity first, acquiring prime assets before nominal prices adjust.
* **By the time expanded liquidity reaches wage earners, pensioners, and small businesses**, consumer prices and asset valuations have already risen.

The nominal figures in retail bank accounts remain unchanged, but their purchasing power is eroded. Currency dilution functions as an implicit, regressive wealth tax that transfers purchasing power from productive labor to financial intermediaries.

## 5. The Collapse of the Final Myth: The Illusion of "Information Production" and Credit Screening

In orthodox financial economics (such as Relationship Banking theory and Douglas Diamond’s seminal 1984 paper), the ultimate defense of commercial banking is **"Information Production"**—the reduction of asymmetric information.
The textbook claim is that market investors cannot evaluate borrower quality; therefore, loan officers embedded in long-term client relationships generate indispensable proprietary data ("screening expertise") to allocate capital efficiently.

Examining modern empirical reality and data science completely shatters this myth.

### ① Algorithmic Dominance over Standardized Underwriting
Retail and SME credit underwriting has been thoroughly automated by predictive algorithms—logistic regression, gradient boosting (LightGBM/XGBoost), and continuous transaction telemetry. Machine models assess repayment probability far more accurately, neutrally, and cheaply than human loan committees. Human intervention introduces emotional bias, nepotism, and bureaucratic CYA rituals (*Ringisho*). In standardized credit risk assessment, commercial banks possess zero unique competitive advantage.

### ② The Absence of Screening: Absolute Reliance on Collateral and Hostages
In non-standard corporate lending, do banks deploy sophisticated qualitative insight? The actual practice in Japan and legacy banking reveals the exact opposite.
Commercial banks do not underwrite enterprise potential; they demand:
* **Real estate collateral** (foreclosing land in case of default)
* **Personal executive guarantees** (*Keieisha Hoshō*—holding the entrepreneur's home and family livelihood hostage)
* **Public credit guarantees** (offloading 80–100% of default losses onto state taxpayers via Credit Guarantee Corporations)

Banks do not absorb risk through superior information; they shift risk entirely onto personal hostages, hard collateral, and public subsidies.

### ③ A Catastrophic Track Record in Future Valuation
When evaluating emerging growth sectors, the historical track record of banking "expertise" over the past four decades is disastrous:
* **The Bubble Era**: Reckless over-lending to speculative real estate developers and golf courses, creating mountains of toxic non-performing loans.
* **The IT Defeat**: Complete blindness to intangible assets (software and intellectual property), rejecting credit to software startups due to lack of physical collateral, thereby suffocating Japan's digital economy.
* **Self-Inflicted Losses**: When lending margins dried up under zero rates, regional banks scrambled into structured credit products and foreign duration bets, accumulating massive balance-sheet impairments.

Far from being elite judges of capital allocation, bank credit committees have functioned as sub-par noise generators lagging broader market intelligence.

### Confusing Historical Genealogy with Normative Architecture
Apologists often invoke history: "Historically, banking emerged when goldsmiths began lending out paper receipts against physical deposits. Credit creation and payment settlement evolved as twins. Therefore, they must remain integrated."

This is a classic genetic fallacy. **How a system historically originated is completely irrelevant to how it should be engineered under modern technological constraints.**
The fact that early steam locomotives integrated coal burners and passenger compartments does not justify prohibiting the separation of power systems and passenger cabins in a modern maglev. In the digital age, payment settlement and balance management can be implemented with mathematical purity, completely detached from fractional-reserve credit risk. Appealing to medieval history is merely an excuse for entrenched inertia.

Banks do not endure because of superior screening intellect or historical inevitability. **They endure solely because state-enforced statutory privileges—central bank access monopolies, free-riding on deposit insurance, and statutory zero-interest mandates—shield them from competitive market discipline.**

## 6. Why the System Persists: A Public Choice Diagnosis

Why does this institutional rent structure resist political reform? Public Choice Theory provides the structural explanation:

### ① Concentrated Benefits vs. Dispersed Costs
* **Concentrated Bank Benefits**: Low deposit betas, zero-interest checking accounts, and payment monopolies generate hundreds of billions in concentrated annual profit for the financial sector. The industry has overwhelming incentives to invest in lobbying and regulatory capture via banking federations.
* **Dispersed Depositor Costs**: For any individual depositor or business, the uncollected interest or transaction fee amounts to a minor sum. No single citizen has the economic incentive to spend months organizing political campaigns to eliminate checking account rate restrictions.

### ② Regulatory Capture and the Revolving Door (*Amakudari*)
For regulators at the Ministry of Finance, the Financial Services Agency, and the Bank of Japan, commercial banks represent vital policy partners during public service and lucrative executive appointments (*Amakudari*) after retirement. Bureaucrats face disincentives to dismantle the profit engines of institutions that guarantee their future livelihoods.

### ③ Credit Asymmetry and Corporate Silence
Corporate executives subject to zero-interest checking accounts do not protest because commercial banks control their credit lines, commercial paper facilities, and settlement access. Publicly challenging bank privileges risks private retaliation in loan renewals and credit ratings.

### ④ Obfuscation Through Technocratic Jargon
By cloaking simple statutory privileges in complex terminology—"prudential liquidity ratios," "statutory interest limitations on demand settlement accounts," "macroprudential backstops"—the establishment creates the illusion that these mechanisms are technical necessities beyond democratic scrutiny.

## 7. Protocols for Structural Reform and Individual EXIT

How can this parasitic rent architecture be dismantled?

### ① The Chicago Plan: Complete Functional Separation (Narrow Banking)
The cleanest structural reform remains the Narrow Banking model:
* **100% Reserve Backing for Payments**: Deposit-taking institutions must back all payment balances 100% with central bank reserves or short-term treasury bills. Central bank interest is passed through entirely to depositors, outlawing unearned spreads.
* **Market-Disciplined Risk Lending**: Lending institutions must raise capital via equity or investment funds without public deposit guarantees. In the event of default, institutions face liquidation under market discipline without taxpayer bailouts.

By separating payment utilities from speculative risk-taking, unearned privileges and systemic moral hazards are simultaneously eliminated.

### ② Opening Central Bank Rails to Objective Safety Standards
Rather than rationing central bank access through omnibus bank charters, regulators should establish objective prudential criteria (100% liquidity backing, segregated accounts, audited proof of reserves, AML) permitting any compliant technology provider to connect directly to settlement rails.

### ③ Personal Protocols for Individual EXIT
Because the state-banking apparatus is structurally co-dependent, top-down legislative reform will not originate from within. The most effective countermeasure is individual and institutional EXIT:

1. **Minimize Idle Working Capital in Commercial Accounts**  
   Treat bank accounts strictly as temporary plumbing for day-to-day transactions. Excess liquidity should not be left as free fuel for bank spreads.
2. **Migrate Wealth into Mathematically Verifiable Assets (Bitcoin)**  
   To defend purchasing power against the Cantillon Effect, store capital in an unalterable, cryptographically enforced protocol with a fixed supply cap (21 million) immune to bureaucratic debasement.
3. **Adopt Direct Peer-to-Peer Settlement Rails**  
   Progressively utilize permissionless cryptographic settlement networks that operate independently of legacy correspondent bank tollgates.
