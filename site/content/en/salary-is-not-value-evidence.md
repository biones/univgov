## 2. Subjective Surveys Are Garbage: Measure "Objective Functional Replaceability"

Since David Graeber published *Bullshit Jobs* in 2018, sociologists and labor economists have attempted to test his claims empirically.

Yet mainstream academic studies (such as Soffia, Wood, & Burchell, 2022 using the European Working Conditions Survey, or Dur & van Lent, 2019 analyzing 47 countries) suffer from a fatal methodological flaw. They rely on **subjective self-reporting surveys**, asking workers: *"Do you feel your job is socially useful?"* Finding that only 5% to 20% of workers answer "rarely or never," these researchers concluded that bullshit jobs are relatively rare—and noted that bankers, bureaucrats, and corporate lawyers almost always report that their jobs are highly meaningful, whereas assembly-line workers and cleaners feel the most alienated.

Some academics actually cited this to claim that finance and bureaucracy are not full of bullshit jobs. **This is a textbook example of academic gullibility.**

### Why the Biggest Parasites Always Believe They Are Essential

If sanitation workers, logistics drivers, or power-grid technicians vanished tomorrow, cities would drown in waste and civilization would halt within days. Their work is 100% objectively essential. When they report low meaning on surveys, they are expressing *Marxian alienation* (low autonomy, harsh conditions, low pay)—not functional uselessness.

Conversely, if you ask a banker earning \$150,000 a year structuring regulatory arbitrage, or a bureaucrat drafting subsidy application guidelines, *"Is your job essential to society?"*, 100 out of 100 will puff out their chests and answer "Yes!" Human psychology is governed by **cognitive dissonance reduction**: the higher the salary and prestige attached to a position, the more fiercely the occupant's brain invents elaborate jargon and self-justifications to convince themselves that society would collapse without their stamp.

Recent organizational research on **"Functional Stupidity"** (Alvesson & Spicer), **"Sludge"** (Cass Sunstein's quantification of administrative friction), and the **Generative AI Bullshit Paradox**—where senders use AI to generate 20-page reports in ten seconds, and recipients use AI to summarize them back into three bullet points—confirms how organizations endlessly manufacture self-referential busywork while participants insist they are doing vital work.

Therefore, to identify bullshit jobs scientifically, we must ignore workers' subjective feelings and apply a single cold, objective standard: **Functional Replaceability**.

## 3. [Empirical Evidence #1: Banking & Finance] How Bitcoin and Natural Experiments Expose the Redundancy of Bankers Worldwide

### Evidence A: Thomas Philippon's Proof of the Unchanging 2% Parasitic Spread (1880–2010s)

In a landmark study published in the *American Economic Review* (2015), New York University economist Thomas Philippon measured the unit cost of financial intermediation in the United States over 130 years, from the 1880s to the 2010s.

In the 1880s, finance relied on horse-drawn carriages, paper ledgers, and human clerks calculating by hand. Over the next century, telephones, mainframes, the internet, and fiber optics reduced the technical cost of recording and transmitting information by orders of magnitude.

Naturally, the cost of intermediating capital should have plummeted. Instead, Philippon discovered a stunning anomaly:

* **The unit cost extracted by the financial sector to intermediate \$1 of capital into the real economy remained virtually unchanged at roughly 1.5% to 2% for 130 straight years.**

Why did a century of computing revolutions fail to make finance cheaper for society? Because every dollar saved by information technology was captured internally by the financial cartel—consumed by **zero-sum high-frequency trading games, complex derivatives churning, regulatory compliance rituals, and sky-high compensation for bankers**.

### Evidence B: The 1970 Irish Bank Strike (A 6-Month Total Closure Natural Experiment)

What happens if a modern country's commercial banks actually shut down? History already ran the experiment.

From May to November 1970, a labor strike shut down approximately 85% of all commercial bank branches in Ireland for **six and a half months**. Cash withdrawals, check clearing, and bank lending froze. Economists predicted immediate economic collapse.

Instead, as documented by Oxford economist Anthony Murphy and Central Bank of Ireland researchers, **the Irish economy did not collapse—it continued to grow normally throughout the strike.**

How? The moment the centralized banking tollbooth vanished, Irish citizens and businesses spontaneously formed a **decentralized peer-to-peer credit clearing network**. People endorsed checks directly to one another as circulating media of exchange, and **over 11,000 local pubs and retail shops acted as decentralized trust nodes**, using their direct local knowledge of customers to clear checks and provide liquidity. For half a year, without bank clerks or headquarters, commerce and payroll functioned smoothly.

If citizens in 1970 could route around a total banking shutdown using paper checks in pubs, consider how trivial replacement is today with cryptographic protocols.

Why does an international bank transfer (via SWIFT and correspondent banking) still take days and cost exorbitant fees? Because banks maintain isolated, proprietary legacy ledgers that require bucket-brigade reconciliation and manual compliance checks across multiple intermediary institutions.
