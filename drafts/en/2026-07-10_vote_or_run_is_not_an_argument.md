# "If you have a problem, go vote. If you don't like it, run for office" is Not a Valid Argument Against Systemic Criticism

Whenever policies or laws are criticized, someone inevitably says:

> This was decided by elected representatives, so it reflects the will of the people. If you have a problem with it, go vote, or run for office yourself.

But this is not a counterargument. It simply substitutes the explanation "the current system has an input device" for the proof that "its output is valid."

I am not saying elections contain absolutely zero public will. However, what goes into them is a rough, noisy signal that compresses candidates, political parties, name recognition, partisanship, the economy, scandals, organized voting blocks, and strategic voting into a single ballot. We cannot use this as a basis to claim that passed laws or individual public expenditures are "supported by the will of the people."

## You Cannot Identify Policy-Specific Support from a Single Vote

It is easier to understand if we view this as a statistical problem.

The observation left by voters in each election is, in principle, a single category: "Which candidate or party did you choose?" Meanwhile, the options bundle together a multitude of distinct elements—taxes, welfare, nuclear power, diplomacy, education, regulation, and the evaluation of the candidate themselves.

Even if someone votes for Party A, it is impossible to know whether they agreed with the tax cuts, appreciated the diplomatic policy, or merely wanted Party B to lose. We cannot deduce that they supported every promise of Party A, nor that they approved of policies added after the election.

This is not merely an issue of "imperfect reflection of the people's will." It is an observational design problem: **you cannot identify support for individual policies within a bundled choice.** When the winning side retroactively claims, "This policy also gained the mandate of the people," they are drawing conclusions that the data simply does not support.

Furthermore, voters are not a random sample extracted from the population. The very act of voting is self-selected and correlates with age, income, organizational mobilization, and political interest. The result—"the most popular among the presented candidates by those who voted"—is entirely different from the policy-specific preferences of the entire nation.

## The "Will of the People" May Not Exist as a Single Entity Before Aggregation

Social choice theory has shown that simply gathering individual rankings does not automatically reveal a consistent "will of the people."

With three or more options, a cycle can occur where A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. Arrow's Impossibility Theorem also demonstrates that there is no general method for converting individual preferences into a consistently valid social ranking while satisfying a set of plausible conditions.

This does not mean "democracy has been mathematically completely denied." By relaxing conditions or using information beyond simple rankings, different aggregation methods can be created. The critical point is this: **election results are not a "will of the people" discovered in nature, but an institutional output generated by how choices are framed and aggregated.**

If you change who the candidates are, what the issues are, or the electoral system used for aggregation, a different "will of the people" could be produced from the exact same population.

## Voting is Not Policy Impact Verification

What an election tells us is which candidate was comparatively chosen at a specific point in time. It does not tell us how many people a policy saved, how much it cost, or how effective it was compared to alternatives.

If we want to know the validity of a policy, what we actually need is the following information:

- Objectives and evaluation metrics
- Comparison between subjects and non-subjects
- Counterfactuals of not implementing the policy
- Effect size and uncertainty
- Distribution of benefits and costs
- Cost-effectiveness comparison with other measures
- Ex-post verification and conditions for abolition if ineffective

Elections measure none of these. In fact, the Japanese government itself describes Evidence-Based Policy Making (EBPM) as "clarifying policy objectives, planning based on rational evidence, and utilizing data related to policy effects." This means that, even following the government's own stated principles, "being elected" is not evidence of policy effectiveness.

A majority vote does not change the acceleration of gravity, nor does it alter the efficacy of a drug. Similarly, whether a subsidy program is cost-effective, whether regulations reduced harm, or whether public facilities were more useful than alternatives are questions that must be verified by data, not by voting.

## There is No Premise That Only the Elected Act for the Public Good

The important point of public choice theory is not to treat politicians and bureaucrats as villains. It is to refuse the assumption that ordinary humans—who are assumed to have self-interest in the market—magically transform into calculating machines for the public good the moment they enter a legislative building or government office.

Politicians have an incentive for re-election, parties for seats, bureaucratic organizations for budget and authority, and industry groups for increasing their own benefits. In policies where a small industry gains immense benefits per capita while the costs are spread thinly and widely among taxpayers, the beneficiaries organize and lobby intensely, while the benefit for an average citizen to investigate and oppose it individually is negligible.

In this structure, even without blatant cash bribery, policies can be skewed through donations, campaign support, petitions, amakudari (revolving door appointments), information provision, advisory council appointments, and the back-and-forth movement of personnel between industry and administration. International IDEA also notes that if political finance is not properly controlled, money and private interests can dictate political outcomes rather than the will of the people.

Therefore, "they passed an election, so it's not collusion" does not hold true. Rather, precisely because the system requires funding, organization, and exposure to win elections, we must scrutinize the incentives that breed exchanges with donors and organized voting blocs.

## "Go Vote" Shifts the System's Flaws Onto Individual Lack of Effort

In a large-scale election, the probability of a single vote altering the outcome is minuscule. However, the time and cost to investigate all policies are borne by the individual. Therefore, the fact that many voters do not study policies in depth is a rational response to the incentives created by the system.

If everyone studied harder and voted, things might improve slightly. But the fact that candidates present policies in bundles, that agenda-setting is controlled by parties and media, that individual policies cannot be bound after the election, and that effectiveness verification trails the election cycle—none of this is fixed by adding one more enthusiastic voter.

What can be mitigated by individual effort is distinct from what is valid as an institutional design. Telling someone who criticizes a broken measuring instrument to "try harder when using that instrument" does not eliminate its margin of error.

To begin with, criticizing policies, investigating, speaking out, petitioning, demanding information disclosure, and verifying effects are all forms of political participation to monitor power. If we treat elections as the only legitimate input and shut down all other criticism as "complaints from the losing side," it is tantamount to saying we should stop monitoring those in power between elections.

## "If You Don't Like It, Run for Office" is an Even Sloppier Counterargument

A consumer does not need to start a car company to point out a defect in a car. A patient does not need to become a doctor to criticize the healthcare system. Similarly, a citizen's right to criticize the parliamentary system has nothing to do with their ability to run for an election.

Running for office requires time, funding, name recognition, organization, and professional sacrifice. If only those who can bear these burdens are allowed to criticize the system, that in itself acts as a barrier to entry, protecting incumbents and established parties.

Furthermore, if the issue at hand is not "the selection of the legislators" but rather "the system that delegates extensive policy-making in bundles to a minority known as legislators," participating in that game as a player is not a rebuttal. It is like responding to a criticism that the rules of soccer are flawed by saying, "Then become a soccer player."

## Elections Grant Limited Procedural Authority, Not Truth or a Blank Check

Elections have an important function: they allow us to change those in power without violence and force regimes to step down at regular intervals. There is no need to deny this.

However, it is primarily a procedure to choose and dismiss leaders.

- That individual policies are supported by the public
- That policies are effective
- That they are more cost-effective than alternatives
- That they are free from conflicts of interest and financial influence
- That the burden on the minority is justified

None of these can be derived from election results alone.

Winning an election should strictly mean "you have obtained the qualification to hold that office for a certain period under current rules." It is not a blank check that dictates "whatever we do is the will of the people" or "it is correct because it became a law."

## What We Need is Not to Erase the Will of the People, But to Narrow Where It Is Used

Policy judgments contain a mixture of at least three types of information.

1. **Estimation of Facts** — Accident probability, health impacts, policy effects, costs, etc.
2. **Understanding of Interests** — Who receives the benefits, and who bears the costs and risks?
3. **Remaining Value Choices** — Priorities and acceptable thresholds that remain divided even when looking at the same facts and interests.

The one-person-one-vote election mashes all three together and forces them into a single candidate choice. Instead, it would be better to update facts through scientific knowledge and data, visualize interests through networks of employment, donations, contracts, and revolving doors, and measure value choices only within the necessary scope.

Fear or simple preference need not become an unconditional veto. Risk tolerance and willingness to pay can be quantified—after defining the scope of impact—as willingness-to-pay amounts, preference intensity, distribution, and externalities. If we just say "we will spend massive public funds because some people are anxious" without measuring the degree, the loudness of voices alone will dictate the cost.

AI and social media analysis can serve as auxiliary tools to continuously observe these value choices and daily struggles. While raw data from X (formerly Twitter) cannot be simply called a representative sample of the nation, it may have the potential to pick up issue-specific reactions, minority opinions, conflicts of interest, and signs of organized manipulation more finely than a single vote every few years. This is not a religion claiming "AI is always right," but a research agenda to compare elections, random sampling surveys, social media estimations, and actual policy outcomes to verify which method captures what and to what extent.

And fundamentally, if we reduce the scope of what the government decides, the conflicts of values that must be aggregated will also decrease. Rather than an enormous system where one vote by the entire population decides everything—and then representatives and industry groups distribute the spoils—it is far more modern to limit the government's role, break down necessary decisions issue by issue, and return verifiable parts back to data.

## Conclusion

The phrase "Go vote. If you don't like it, run for office" only explains how to participate in the current game. What we are questioning is whether that game is structurally capable of accurately measuring the people's will, balancing interests, and selecting effective policies.

Elections contain a trace of the public will. So what?

You cannot identify policy-specific support from a single vote, results change based on options and aggregation methods, winners have their own self-interests, and policy effects are not measured. Simply calling that output "the will of the people" does not repair a single flaw in the system.

Voting is a technology to peacefully change those in power. But it is not a technology to prove the correctness of policies. There is no reason to treat a governance method that has lasted for a long time as if it were a law of nature or a final, perfected form.

Before pressuring critics of the system to run for office, the system itself should be compared to alternatives and evaluated with data.

## Short Reply Version

> Just because elections contain a fraction of the public will does not mean individual policies are supported by the public, effective, or cost-efficient. You cannot identify support for individual policies from a single vote that bundles candidates and dozens of policies together. Furthermore, options are created by political parties, and the elected and bureaucrats have their own self-interests involving re-election, budgets, and industry ties. "Go vote" is merely an explanation of how to participate in the current system, not a rebuttal to the system's aggregation errors or rent-seeking. "Run for office" is tantamount to telling someone to start a manufacturing company if they point out a product defect. While elections are a technology to change those in power, they are not a technology to prove the correctness of policies.
